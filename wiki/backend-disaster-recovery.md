# Backend disaster recovery

Q10 extends the recovery intent in [[backend-implementation-proposal]] to an entire hosting-system outage while retaining a small codebase. In Q11, responding to the entire-cloud-provider outage scenario, the owner required recovery within one hour and preferred recovery in under fifteen minutes. Provider-wide unavailability is therefore in scope. No vendor, replication design, budget increase, or data-loss allowance is selected here.

Q12 accepts restoring live tournament functions first to avoid delayed rounds and lost user trust: target under fifteen minutes for live operations, with reporting and large exports restored within one hour. Q72 later removed the former $500 monthly infrastructure cap and chose durability first: zero loss of acknowledged writes and automatic safe cross-provider failover remain mandatory.

## Failure boundary

Multi-region deployments distribute infrastructure across regions. Multicloud disaster recovery places production and recovery with different providers and adds feasibility, operational, networking, and cost considerations. **Docket inference:** another region within the same provider cannot independently cover an outage of that entire provider; recovery infrastructure and its essential dependencies must remain usable outside the failed boundary. [Google Cloud business continuity patterns](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/hybrid-multicloud-patterns-and-practices/business-continuity-patterns)

Q75 keeps every production voting replica, witness, backup, telemetry store, and object copy in US regions even while they span providers. Q73 requires writes to stop whenever a safe consensus majority cannot be proven. Q74 permits a success receipt only after the protected majority durably commits the validated and authorized transaction, and Q76 requires strong reads for live state and authority. These choices trade write availability and WAN latency for the accepted zero-loss guarantee; a minority partition cannot continue serving writes.

Recovery point objective (RPO) describes allowable data loss; recovery time objective (RTO) describes allowable downtime. They are separate requirements. [AWS recovery concepts](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/guidance/latest/deploying-cross-region-disaster-recovery-with-aws-elastic-disaster-recovery/core-concepts.html)

## Options and tradeoffs

The following codebase assessments are Docket design judgments, not measured implementation sizes. Each option assumes recovery copies outside the covered failure boundary.

| Approach | Acknowledged-write protection | Codebase and operational tradeoff |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Independent backups and restore | Recovery reaches the latest usable backup/log copy; later acknowledged changes may be lost. | Small application change; restoration, deployment, and routing work increase downtime. |
| Asynchronous standby | Recent commits can be missing from the surviving standby. Low replication lag is not zero-loss protection. | Same application implementation; additional live database, replication monitoring, promotion, and recovery procedures. |
| Synchronous durable standby | Can preserve acknowledged commits through loss of the primary site when a required durable copy survives outside it. | Same application implementation remains possible; every protected commit depends on remote durability, adding latency and potential write unavailability. |

Backup/restore and standby are distinct recovery strategies, with different preparation and recovery costs. Replication alone does not replace historical backups against destructive changes or corruption. [AWS disaster recovery strategies](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/whitepapers/latest/disaster-recovery-workloads-on-aws/disaster-recovery-options-in-the-cloud.html)

PostgreSQL streaming replication defaults to asynchronous operation. Synchronous replication waits for required standby acknowledgements; the network round trip increases commit latency. Losing the required standby connectivity prevents protected commits from being acknowledged until that requirement can again be met. [PostgreSQL standby replication](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/warm-standby.html)

With configured synchronous standbys, `synchronous_commit=on` waits for remote durable storage. `remote_write` is weaker because a standby operating-system crash can lose unflushed data. Setting `on` without synchronous standbys only ensures local durability. The success receipt must follow the protected commit; zero loss is conditional on the defined failures and surviving durable storage, not an absolute guarantee. [PostgreSQL WAL settings](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/runtime-config-wal.html)

## Keeping Docket small

**Accepted application boundary under Q77:** retain one TypeScript domain implementation and one logical CockroachDB Enterprise cluster. Deploy the same API/worker release in every protected provider. Keep replication, quorum, and leadership inside the consensus infrastructure; application code needs endpoint recovery, bounded whole-transaction retries, duplicate-command protection, durable background jobs, and polling-based live-event discovery. No Rust crate is introduced.

Promotion must prevent the old primary from accepting writes. PostgreSQL requires external failure detection/promotion coordination and recommends tested procedures; running two conflicting primaries can cause data loss. [PostgreSQL failover](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/warm-standby-failover.html)

## Verified provider constraint conflict

Official-source research on 2026-09-05 did not find a budget-tier managed PostgreSQL offering that documents synchronous cross-provider durability, automatic fenced promotion, and operation inside Docket's total $500 monthly cap. PostgreSQL distinguishes synchronous protection of committed transactions from asynchronous replication that can lose recent commits, and safe promotion still requires external failure detection and fencing. [PostgreSQL high availability](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/high-availability.html) [PostgreSQL failover](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/warm-standby-failover.html)

The reviewed affordable managed options do not close that gap. Render documents asynchronous same-region HA that may lose the latest writes; Supabase documents asynchronous read replicas; Aurora Global Database and AlloyDB cross-region replication are asynchronous and remain inside one cloud provider. Crunchy Bridge documents cross-provider replicas, but its published promotion flow is an operator detach operation rather than a documented automatic fenced failover. [Render high availability](https://render.com/docs/postgresql-high-availability) [Supabase read replicas](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/platform/read-replicas) [Aurora Global Database disaster recovery](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonRDS/latest/AuroraUserGuide/aurora-global-database-disaster-recovery.html) [AlloyDB cross-region replication](https://docs.cloud.google.com/alloydb/docs/cross-region-replication/about-cross-region-replication) [Crunchy Bridge replicas](https://docs.crunchybridge.com/how-to/replicas)

A provisional budget-oriented topology using primary and recovery compute at different providers, Crunchy Bridge HA plus a smaller cross-provider replica, independent object backups, and health-based routing was estimated from published rates at roughly $419–$485 per month before load balancing, full observability, excess egress, and measured usage. It therefore lacks enough verified headroom to claim the hard cap, and its smaller recovery replica has not been shown to meet Docket's accepted recovery load. [Crunchy Data pricing](https://www.crunchydata.com/pricing) [Railway pricing](https://docs.railway.com/pricing/plans) [Fly.io pricing](https://fly.io/docs/about/pricing/)

This created a product decision, not an implementation detail. Q72 resolved it in favor of durability: the $500 cap is removed, while zero-loss acknowledged writes and automatic safe cross-provider recovery remain mandatory. Docket will pursue an enterprise managed or custom consensus architecture. Q73–Q76 further require fail-closed quorum loss, majority-commit acknowledgements, US-only production data, and strong live reads. Provider selection remains open, and no topology may claim compliance until its quorum, fencing or minority exclusion, latency, recovery capacity, jurisdiction, and failure behavior are verified.

## Enterprise consensus candidates

Current official-source comparison recommends a contractually supported CockroachDB Enterprise cluster spanning three cloud providers as the leading candidate. CockroachDB documents Raft majority commits, automatic leadership recovery, minority-side write exclusion, and region-survival operation with zero-data-loss language. A proposed proof topology uses three US provider regions, three nodes per provider, and five voting replicas per range placed 2+2+1 so loss of either two-replica provider leaves a 3-of-5 quorum. The exact placement remains a candidate until vendor review and latency tests. [CockroachDB replication](https://www.cockroachlabs.com/docs/stable/architecture/replication-layer.html) [CockroachDB region survival](https://docs.cockroachlabs.com/docs/v26.3/multiregion-survival-goals) [CockroachDB multi-cloud](https://www.cockroachlabs.com/blog/why-multicloud-database/)

The standard CockroachDB Cloud SLA describes multi-region service within the applicable cloud provider, so catalog cloud service must not be assumed to cover total provider loss. Procurement should first request a vendor-managed or BYOC contract explicitly spanning AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud; if unavailable, the qualifying path is self-hosted CockroachDB Enterprise with vendor support and professional services. Cost is quote-based and expected to include nine always-on database nodes, inter-cloud networking and egress, enterprise licensing, support, and three-provider application capacity. [CockroachDB Cloud SLA](https://www.cockroachlabs.com/cloud-terms-and-conditions/cockroachcloud-technical-service-level-agreement/) [CockroachDB licensing](https://www.cockroachlabs.com/docs/stable/licensing-faqs)

CockroachDB is PostgreSQL-wire-compatible rather than PostgreSQL itself. Node.js `pg` remains usable, Drizzle provides a CockroachDB dialect, and pg-boss documents a CockroachDB backend. Application changes include whole-transaction retries for SQLSTATE `40001`, Cockroach-aware migrations and UUID identities, and replacing PostgreSQL `NOTIFY` with bounded durable-event polling or a proven changefeed. [Drizzle CockroachDB](https://orm.drizzle.team/docs/cockroach/get-started-cockroach) [pg-boss database backends](https://pgboss.io/database-backends)

YugabyteDB Anywhere is the fallback candidate because it documents synchronous Raft replication and universes spanning AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud, but it is customer-operated, Drizzle lacks a documented Yugabyte-specific integration, `LISTEN/NOTIFY` support is not a stable baseline, and pg-boss marks Yugabyte support partial. EDB PGD preserves more PostgreSQL fidelity but requires careful non-degrading quorum-commit configuration and customer-managed routing; its standard managed service does not document the required cross-provider automatic behavior. Plain PostgreSQL with Patroni leaves the greatest fencing and edge-case proof burden and is not recommended for this absolute promise. [YugabyteDB multi-cloud](https://docs.yugabyte.com/stable/yugabyte-platform/create-deployments/create-universe-multi-cloud/) [YugabyteDB replication](https://docs.yugabyte.com/stable/architecture/docdb-replication/replication/) [EDB PGD commit scopes](https://www.enterprisedb.com/docs/pgd/latest/commit-scopes/commit-scope-rules/) [Patroni replication modes](https://patroni.readthedocs.io/en/latest/replication_modes.html)

No candidate is accepted merely from documentation. A proof must exercise all schemas and migrations, pg-boss behavior, `40001` retries, 5,000 clients and accepted peak load, loss and isolation of each provider during commit, loss of two providers with fail-closed behavior, WAN delay and packet loss, rejoin and rebalancing, dependency failures, upgrades, and independent-backup restoration. Contract language must explicitly cover one logical database across three providers, RPO 0 after loss of any one, automatic minority-safe election, recovery time, supported placement, and responsibility boundaries.

## Cross-cloud edge and object-storage evidence

Cloudflare Enterprise Load Balancing can use public origins across providers, perform frequent health monitoring, and proxy WebSockets without application-specific edge code. An existing WebSocket does not migrate when an origin or the edge connection changes, so Docket's snapshot-first reconnect path remains mandatory. Cloudflare would be a common edge dependency; adding a second automatic proxy plane would require separate routing and failure proof. [Cloudflare load balancing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/load-balancing/) [Cloudflare monitors](https://developers.cloudflare.com/load-balancing/monitors/create-monitor/) [Cloudflare WebSockets](https://developers.cloudflare.com/network/websockets/)

DNS failover changes later name resolutions but cannot move existing HTTP or WebSocket connections, and resolver caching can delay adoption of new answers. This supports Q88's decision not to represent dual-DNS configuration as seamless edge recovery. [Route 53 DNS failover](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/Route53/latest/DeveloperGuide/dns-failover.html) [Route 53 health checks](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/Route53/latest/DeveloperGuide/dns-failover-determining-health-of-endpoints.html)

Native object replication is asynchronous: Amazon S3 replication, Azure Blob object replication, and Google Storage Transfer Service do not provide a generic synchronous three-provider acknowledgement boundary. Therefore asynchronous replication cannot establish zero loss for an irreplaceable object acknowledged immediately before its source provider disappears. The next storage decision must choose explicit synchronous writes and failure behavior; this is an architectural inference from the documented replication semantics. [S3 replication](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/replication.html) [Azure object replication](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/storage/blobs/object-replication-overview) [Google event-driven transfers](https://docs.cloud.google.com/storage-transfer/docs/event-driven-transfers)

## Encryption and upload-integrity evidence

S3 encrypts new objects by default and Google Cloud Storage always encrypts stored data, using independent provider-managed encryption systems when Docket uploads each copy without application-level ciphertext. Losing one provider's encryption service therefore does not make the other provider's independent copy depend on it. Customer-managed KMS keys can add rotation, audit, and revocation control, but their region, permissions, retained old versions, and deletion become additional operational obligations. [S3 default encryption](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/default-bucket-encryption.html) [Cloud Storage encryption](https://docs.cloud.google.com/storage/docs/encryption) [S3 SSE-KMS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/UsingKMSEncryption.html) [GCS customer-managed keys](https://docs.cloud.google.com/storage/docs/encryption/using-customer-managed-keys)

Encrypting both stored copies once at the application layer while wrapping the data key under only one provider's KMS would make the supposedly independent copy unreadable during that provider's KMS outage. Dual-wrapped envelope encryption can avoid that dependency but requires streaming cryptography, two KMS integrations, authenticated framing, rewrap tooling, and recovery tests. External key managers add another networked availability dependency. The smallest currently recommended design is independent provider-managed encryption, with customer-managed keys added only for a concrete compliance requirement. This is an architectural inference from the documented key models. [Google envelope encryption](https://docs.cloud.google.com/kms/docs/envelope-encryption) [AWS multi-keyrings](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/encryption-sdk/latest/developer-guide/use-multi-keyring.html) [AWS external key-store considerations](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/kms/latest/developerguide/key-store-overview.html) [Google Cloud EKM](https://docs.cloud.google.com/kms/docs/ekm)

S3 presigned uploads can bind supported checksums, GCS records CRC32C, and both providers offer conditional creation that prevents a retry from overwriting an existing immutable object. Docket's private SHA-256 remains the cryptographic content identity; provider checksums and version identifiers provide independent transfer and storage evidence. [S3 presigned uploads](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/using-presigned-url.html) [S3 object integrity](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/checking-object-integrity-upload.html) [GCS signed URLs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/storage/docs/access-control/signing-urls-with-helpers) [GCS data validation](https://docs.cloud.google.com/storage/docs/data-validation) [S3 conditional writes](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/conditional-writes.html) [GCS preconditions](https://docs.cloud.google.com/storage/docs/request-preconditions)

## One hour versus under fifteen minutes

These are product targets, not measured outcomes. The TypeScript domain code can be identical for both. The shorter target primarily requires more readiness: an independently hosted recovery deployment already running, current release and configuration, available keys, prepared routing, safe database promotion, and rehearsed recovery checks. Recovery must not depend on retrieving artifacts or controlling infrastructure through the failed provider.

One hour leaves more time for an operator to initiate recovery and for application capacity to start or scale. Under fifteen minutes favors running standby capacity and faster detection and switchover. Neither timing follows automatically from an architecture label. AWS distinguishes pilot light, which needs additional startup, from warm standby, which already runs a functional deployment. Applying this principle across providers is a Docket design inference, not an AWS cross-provider guarantee. [AWS disaster recovery strategies](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/whitepapers/latest/disaster-recovery-workloads-on-aws/disaster-recovery-options-in-the-cloud.html)

Added repository content is primarily deployment configuration, recovery orchestration, monitoring, and failure tests rather than duplicate tournament rules. Lines of code cannot be estimated responsibly before infrastructure selection. Managed tools can reduce custom code, but cross-provider support must be verified. Faster recovery does not relax the separate requirement to preserve every acknowledged command.

Recommended verification counts from user-visible outage onset until authorized reads and writes work at the required recovery load, including detection, promotion, routing, reconnection, and checks. Q13 selects automatic failover with an immediate Platform Administration alert when a safe switch can be verified. Recovery capacity and the concrete failover mechanism remain open.

Automatic recovery must verify the surviving copy's required durable state and prevent the former primary from accepting writes before resuming writes on the replacement. An unreachable primary alone is insufficient evidence for safe promotion. If safety cannot be established, the system must keep writes unavailable and escalate rather than silently discard acknowledged submissions or create competing writers. Alert delivery does not gate an otherwise safe switch. Recovery tests must cover false alarms, network partitions, interrupted promotion, returning former primaries, client retries, and worker resumption. The automatic-failover decision does not itself authorize automatic failback to the original host.

Q27 requires Platform Administration approval before failback. Recovery continues serving as the active writer while the original system is repaired, synchronized to the authoritative recovery lineage, and checked. The approval records the target, synchronization and health evidence, expected interruption, rollback plan, and administrator. Failback must fence the current writer before activating the target and must preserve idempotency and worker state. A failed or partial switch stays visible and alerts Platform Administration; the system does not automatically oscillate between hosts.

Q28 permits planned failback only between rounds or after competition ends. Platform Administration may authorize an active-round exception only if the recovery system is unsafe or unavailable, and must record the condition and expected participant impact. The exception does not weaken the Q27 synchronization, health, fencing, single-writer, or rollback requirements.

Q31 and Q32 keep retry safety and deferred work in the protected consensus state. The idempotency key, canonical request fingerprint, and original result or receipt commit atomically with each accepted write, allowing a client retry after failover to recover the original outcome. Email, push, projection, and export jobs use pg-boss's CockroachDB backend; workers may redeliver after recovery, so handlers remain idempotent and queue leases must expire safely. A separate broker is added only if representative measurements show this smaller design cannot meet an accepted target.

Q33 retains those idempotency results for seven days, well beyond the one-hour recovery limit, while keeping storage bounded through cleanup. Q34–Q36 use at-least-once worker delivery with duplicate suppression, reserve worker capacity for live projections and urgent notices, and defer routine communication and exports behind live work. Live projection failures retry within seconds and alert rapidly; push and email receive three attempts over 24 hours, while reports and exports receive three attempts before staff must explicitly retry. Permanent validation and authorization failures do not enter a retry loop.

Q37 adds a recovery-pressure acceptance fixture: all 5,000 clients reconnect and load fresh authorized snapshots over 60 seconds while the accepted 200-command-per-second burst continues. Jittered client backoff shapes the wave without reducing its total volume. Recovery is not complete merely because the database is promoted; the snapshot path, command path, priority worker lane, and latency and failure gates must remain usable together.

Q38 and Q39 make command recovery deterministic across hosts: the idempotency scope includes Account, Active Role Context, command name and version, and the client action UUID, while duplicate comparison uses the normalized validated-command fingerprint. Q40 bounds each compressed reconnect snapshot at 512 KB so recovery cannot silently shift an unbounded query and network cost onto every returning client. Q41 and Q42 retry failed live projections after approximately one, two, and five seconds, then alert, while using renewable 30-second job leases with roughly ten-second heartbeats so another worker can reclaim abandoned work. Q43 fixes the concurrent command mixture, and Q44 requires urgent push handoff to APNs or FCM within 30 seconds of commit; downstream phone display remains outside Docket's guarantee.

Q45 adds 600 ordinary reads per second to the 60-second peak fixture while commands, live connections, and pairing work continue; the Q37 reconnect snapshots remain additional recovery load. Q46 serves hot recovery reads from small, versioned CockroachDB projections rather than introducing another database. Q47 and Q48 cap ordinary JSON bodies at 256 KB uncompressed and ordinary transactions at a two-second execution limit with a 250-millisecond p95 target, keeping recovery resource use bounded. Q49 sends large transfers directly through short-lived object-storage URLs, but the selected recovery design must ensure that any artifact required for live tournament operation remains reachable outside the failed provider; reports and large exports may follow their accepted one-hour recovery tier.

Q57 makes each live event durable with its authoritative commit. Q77 replaces PostgreSQL `NOTIFY` with bounded CockroachDB polling initially; a changefeed requires separate proof. Q58 requires the recovered WebSocket to authenticate through the Docket Session and bind to one current Active Role Context. Q59 and Q60 close dead connections after two missed 25-second heartbeats and close clients exceeding 1 MiB queued data or 100 events of lag, returning them to bounded snapshot synchronization instead of retaining unbounded recovery state.

Q61 reserves at least 20% of contracted CockroachDB connections for migrations, recovery, and operations while limiting each initial API and worker pool to ten. Q77 removes the dedicated PostgreSQL listener connection. Q62 runs reviewed expand–migrate–contract changes once rather than racing API instances, and Q63 rejects corrupt or unsupported stored records through runtime decoders. Recovery drills must cover polling failure, expired sessions and grants, slow consumers, abandoned pools, partially migrated deployments, serialization retries, and invalid recovered data.

Q64–Q66 retain durable live events for 24 hours, bind opaque cursors to the authorized Account/context/subscription/snapshot, and send small invalidations that cause bounded HTTPS projection fetches. Older or mismatched cursors use a fresh snapshot. Q67 and Q68 limit each Account to five live connections, ten writes per second with a burst of 20, and 30 reads per second with a burst of 60; recovery tests must prove those protections do not prevent the aggregate accepted fixture from completing.

Q69 records correlation-safe structured logs, traces, metrics, queue and connection health, and recovery milestones without sensitive payloads. Raw service telemetry expires after 14 days and aggregate service metrics after 90 days. Q70 requires seven days of point-in-time recovery and daily encrypted backups at an independent provider retained for 30 days, with monthly restore verification and a quarterly complete provider-outage drill. Q71 treats a lost response as an unknown outcome: the client queries the original idempotency key and retries with that same key only when no stored result exists.

The under-fifteen-minute live-operations target, one-hour reporting/export recovery limit, and zero-loss cross-provider durability remain unverified in combination. An enterprise design, deployment quote, load test, and recovery drills must establish whether a proposed topology meets them. [AWS disaster recovery testing](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel_planning_for_recovery_dr_tested.html)

The proposed fast-recovery fixture includes sessions, authorization, current schedules and Pairings, check-in, Judge assignments, Ballot submission, and calculations/publication required to advance rounds. Deferred reporting and export jobs must not consume capacity needed by those functions. This is an engineering interpretation of live tournament functions for fixture design, not a new exemption from domain rules. Infrastructure pricing must include consensus or replication traffic, logs, storage growth, monitoring, failover operation, support, and compute. There is no fixed monthly cap, but cost requires explicit approval and ongoing monitoring; no billing threshold may interrupt an active tournament automatically.
